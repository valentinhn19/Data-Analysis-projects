Reading the tests

The cross-sectional t-test (compare groups in 2025 alone) fails — exactly because elite traditional-bat sluggers drag the comparison group's mean up. But once we control for each player's own 2024 baseline (paired test) and look at who improved (z-test and chi-square), the torpedo signal is clear and significant: 36.8% of torpedo users posted a ≥20% jump in HR/AB versus 4.5% of non-users.

Part 2Applied Workflow

Step 1 — Inspect & clean the data

Started with 82 rows × 20 columns of player-season records. Issues found and resolved:

  • Whitespace: One team value ("NYY ") had trailing whitespace — stripped.
  • Duplicates: 2 fully duplicate rows removed.
  • Impossible value: Batting average of 1.350 (impossible — max is 1.000) flagged and replaced with median.
  • Missing values: 4 missing entries across bat_speed, barrel_pct, hard_hit_pct, k_pct — imputed with column medians (preserves distribution shape without distorting tails).

Cleaned shape: 80 rows × 20 columns.

Step 2 — Transform & engineer features

  • Pivot to wide format: one row per player with both 2024 and 2025 stats side-by-side.
  • Δ features: Computed year-over-year change for every metric (e.g., d_avg_exit_velocity, d_hard_hit_pct).
  • Percent change: d_hr_per_ab_pct = relative change in HR/AB.
  • One-hot encoding: handedness (bats_R, bats_L, bats_S).
  • Binning: age groups (U25, 25–30, 30–35, 35+).
  • Standardization: z-scored 2024 baselines for regression stability.
  • Binary outcome: big_improver = 1 if HR/AB jumped >20%.

Engineered table: 41 players × 51 features.

Step 3 — Exploratory data analysis

The cross-sectional view (top) shows the two groups overlap heavily on raw 2025 metrics. The year-over-year view (bottom) tells the real story: torpedo users' distribution of Δ EV is shifted clearly to the right of zero, and their Δ HR/AB% is dispersed but skewed positive — including several big winners and a couple of losers (Bohm, Contreras), which matches the public reporting.

Step 4 & 5 — Choosing methods & applying models

The right analytical framework here is differences-in-differences logic — each player serves as their own control via 2024 stats. Methods chosen:

  • Paired t-test for within-player change (handles natural variability across hitters).
  • Two-proportion z-test & chi-square for the qualitative outcome of "did this player meaningfully improve?".
  • Multiple linear regression on Δ SLG with bat type as a covariate, to test whether the torpedo effect survives controlling for changes in EV, hard-hit rate, and bat speed.
  • Logistic regression for the binary "big improver" outcome.
  • Two-way ANOVA to check whether the effect depends on handedness.

Step 7 — Interpretation

What the numbers say

The torpedo bat does not uniformly raise every hitter's exit velocity or batting average. What it does — measurably and significantly — is materially increase the probability that a player has a breakout home-run year. Torpedo users were ~8× more likely than non-users to see HR/AB jump >20% (36.8% vs 4.5%, p=0.009). In the regression for Δ SLG, the torpedo coefficient is the only significant predictor (β = +0.055, p < 0.001) once other swing changes are controlled for.

Why the cross-sectional t-test misled

The naive 2025 comparison (Welch t-test, p=0.19) suggested no difference. That's because the traditional-bat group includes the best raw hitters in baseball (Judge, Soto, Ohtani, Alvarez). The bat does its work relative to a hitter's own baseline, not by turning a contact hitter into Aaron Judge.

Heterogeneity is real

Bohm and Contreras both got worse with torpedo bats — consistent with the design's premise. The bat shifts the barrel toward where a specific hitter contacts the ball, so a hitter whose contact point doesn't actually match the new geometry can lose performance. This is a personalized equipment story, not a universal upgrade.

Step 8 — Recommendations & communication

For a hitting coach / front office

  • Bat-fit screening before adoption. Use Statcast contact-point data (where on the bat the player actually hits the ball) to pre-qualify candidates. The losers in this sample were not random — they were hitters whose existing contact patterns didn't match the torpedo's geometry.
  • Target younger, higher-bat-speed hitters for adoption. The logistic model rewards positive Δ bat speed and is age-neutral, but real-world adoption costs (re-learning swing feel) are lower for younger players.
  • Don't expect a uniform OPS lift. Expect ~+0.5 mph EV on average and a meaningfully elevated chance of a power breakout. Plan for ~10–15% of adopters to lose performance.

For a broadcaster / writer

  • The story is not "torpedo bats > traditional bats" in raw output — it's "torpedo bats produce a higher rate of breakout seasons among the players who switch."
  • Frame results as Δ vs. each player's 2024 self, not as group means in 2025.

Limitations

  • Sample size: n=41 players is small. CV variance on the regression R² (±0.31) reflects this.
  • Selection bias: Players who adopted the torpedo bat in 2025 are not a random sample — they were curious, often had a down 2024, and may have made other adjustments simultaneously (offseason training, new hitting coach).
  • Single-season effect: Some of the lift could be regression to the mean from a depressed 2024 baseline. A 2025 vs. career-average comparison would strengthen causal interpretation.
  • Mid-season usage: Several players (e.g., Cal Raleigh) use the torpedo only situationally — clean per-at-bat usage data would let us run a finer-grained analysis.
